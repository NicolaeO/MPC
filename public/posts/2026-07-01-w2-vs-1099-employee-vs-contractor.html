W-2 vs 1099: Employee vs Independent Contractor

Two job offers can both say "$40 an hour" and leave you with very different amounts of money. Whether you are a W-2 employee or a 1099 independent contractor changes who pays your taxes, whether you get benefits, and how much paperwork lands on you. This matters in every field — and especially in trucking, construction, cleaning, delivery and IT contracting, where both arrangements are common.

The Basic Difference

W-2 Employee

Your employer withholds income tax from every paycheck and pays half of your Social Security and Medicare taxes.

You are usually eligible for benefits (health insurance, 401(k) match, paid time off), plus unemployment insurance, workers’ compensation, and minimum-wage and overtime protections.

1099 Contractor

You are running a small business. Nothing is withheld — you pay all taxes yourself, including both halves of Social Security and Medicare.

No benefits, no paid time off, no unemployment insurance. In return you control how and when you work, and you can deduct business expenses.

Self-Employment Tax: The Big Surprise

Employees pay 7.65% for Social Security and Medicare (FICA), and the employer pays another 7.65%. Contractors pay the full 15.3% self-employment tax themselves, on 92.35% of their net profit. The Social Security portion (12.4%) applies up to the annual wage base — $184,500 in 2026 — and the Medicare portion (2.9%) applies to all of it.

Contractors get some relief: you can deduct half of your self-employment tax when calculating income tax, and you may qualify for the qualified business income (QBI) deduction of up to 20% of your business profit. But the cash you owe is still noticeably higher.

What a 1099 Rate Needs to Cover

As a contractor, your rate has to pay for things an employer would normally provide:

  • The extra 7.65% of payroll tax
  • Health insurance — often $400–$700+ a month for an individual on the marketplace without subsidies
  • Retirement savings with no employer match
  • Unpaid vacation, holidays and sick days
  • Equipment, software, phone, and professional insurance
Rule of thumb: a 1099 rate should be roughly 25–40% higher than a comparable W-2 hourly rate just to break even. If a W-2 job pays $40 an hour, a similar contract should pay around $50–$56 an hour.

Quarterly Estimated Taxes

Because nobody withholds tax for you, the IRS expects contractors to pay during the year using Form 1040-ES. The four deadlines are:

  • April 15 — for income earned January–March
  • June 15 — April–May
  • September 15 — June–August
  • January 15 of the next year — September–December

If a date falls on a weekend or holiday, it moves to the next business day. Illinois residents also make state estimated payments with Form IL-1040-ES (Illinois has a flat 4.95% income tax).

Avoid the underpayment penalty: you are generally safe if your payments during the year cover at least 100% of last year’s total tax (110% if your prior-year adjusted gross income was over $150,000), or 90% of this year’s. In your first year, setting aside 25–30% of every payment in a separate savings account is a simple, conservative habit. See the IRS estimated taxes page for details.

Deductible Business Expenses

Contractors report income and expenses on Schedule C. Ordinary and necessary business costs reduce your taxable profit, for example:

  • Business mileage (at the IRS standard mileage rate, updated each year) or actual vehicle costs
  • The business share of your phone and internet
  • Computers, tools, software and subscriptions used for work
  • A home office used regularly and exclusively for business
  • Professional licenses, training, and accounting fees
  • Health insurance premiums, through the self-employed health insurance deduction

Keep every receipt and a mileage log. All income is taxable, even if a client never sends you a 1099 form.

Are You Really a Contractor?

Some companies label workers as contractors to avoid paying taxes and benefits. In general, if the company controls when, where and how you work, provides your tools, and you work only for them, you may legally be an employee. If you think you have been misclassified, you can ask the IRS for a determination with Form SS-8 or contact the Illinois Department of Labor. In Illinois construction, the Employee Classification Act provides extra protection.

If You Are Paid on a 1099

  • Open a separate bank account for business income and expenses
  • Move 25–30% of every payment into a tax savings account
  • Calendar the four estimated tax deadlines — federal and Illinois
  • Track mileage and keep receipts in an app or spreadsheet from day one
  • Get your own health insurance through the marketplace
  • Open a SEP IRA or Solo 401(k) to save for retirement and lower your taxes
  • Talk to a tax professional before your first filing season

Compare Offers on Equal Terms

A W-2 job and a 1099 contract are not comparable by hourly rate alone. Add up the taxes, benefits and time off you would be giving up, and make sure the contract rate covers them. If you choose the 1099 route, treat it like a business from the first day — separate accounts, quarterly payments and good records will save you from a painful April.
