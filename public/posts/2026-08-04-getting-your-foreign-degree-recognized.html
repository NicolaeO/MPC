Getting Your Foreign Degree Recognized

Your diploma from Moldova has real value in the US — but American employers, universities and licensing boards do not know how to read it until it has been translated into US terms. A credential evaluation does exactly that, and getting one early can open doors to better jobs, graduate school and professional licenses.

Who Asks for a Credential Evaluation?

  • Employers — especially for roles that require a bachelor’s degree, government jobs, and work-visa petitions such as the H-1B.
  • Universities and community colleges — for admission to graduate programs or to transfer credits.
  • State licensing boards — for nurses, teachers, accountants, engineers, pharmacists and other regulated professions.
  • Employment-based green card petitions that require a specific degree level.

Two Types of Evaluation

Document-by-Document

Confirms what your degree is equivalent to — for example, "equivalent to a US bachelor’s degree in economics."

Usually enough for: most employers and immigration purposes. Cheaper and faster.

Course-by-Course

Lists every course you took with US semester credits, US grades and an overall GPA.

Usually required by: universities, community colleges and most licensing boards.

Check before you pay: ask the employer, school or board which type of evaluation they need and which evaluation companies they accept. Some licensing boards only accept one specific service, and paying for the wrong one means paying twice.

Choosing an Evaluation Service

Most US institutions accept evaluations from members of NACES (the National Association of Credential Evaluation Services). Two of the most widely used are World Education Services (WES) and Educational Credential Evaluators (ECE). Expect to pay roughly $100–$300 or more depending on the type of report and delivery speed, and allow a few weeks after all of your documents arrive.

How Moldovan Degrees Usually Compare

Moldova joined the Bologna Process in 2005, so most modern degrees follow the European bachelor’s–master’s structure, which US evaluators understand well. Typical results — though every evaluation depends on your specific program:

Moldovan credential Common US equivalency
Licență — 4 years (240 ECTS) Bachelor’s degree
Licență — 3 years (180 ECTS) Evaluated case by case; may be treated as three years of undergraduate study
Master after a bachelor’s (90–120 ECTS) Often a master’s degree, especially when the total study is five or more years
Older 5-year "specialist" diploma Often a bachelor’s degree or higher, depending on content

Documents to Gather From Home

  • Your diploma and diploma supplement (suplimentul la diplomă) or academic transcript with every course, hours or credits, and grades.
  • Official copies sent directly by your university — many evaluators require transcripts to arrive in a sealed envelope from the institution or through verified electronic delivery. Contact the university’s secretariat early; this step often takes the longest.
  • Certified English translations — complete, word-for-word translations of anything not in English.
  • Proof of name changes if your name appears differently across documents — for example, Cyrillic-era records or different spellings of diacritics such as "Ș" and "Ş".
  • An apostille, if a board asks for one. Moldova is part of the Hague Apostille Convention, so documents can be apostilled in Chișinău.
Tip: if you are still in Moldova — or have family there who can help — request extra sealed copies of your transcripts now. Getting them later from abroad is much harder.

Licensed Professions Need More Than an Evaluation

For regulated professions, the evaluation is only the first step. Each state sets its own rules; here is the general path:

Profession Typical US path
Registered nurse Credentials review (often through CGFNS), an English test if required, the NCLEX-RN exam, then a state license (IDFPR in Illinois)
Physician ECFMG certification, USMLE exams, and almost always a US residency program before full licensure
Engineer Most engineering jobs do not require a license. For a PE license: the FE exam, supervised experience, then the PE exam — see NCEES
Accountant (CPA) An education evaluation, the CPA exam and work experience — see NASBA
Teacher A course-by-course evaluation and a state teaching license — in Illinois, through ISBE
Lawyer Most states require a US law degree; a few, such as New York, let foreign-trained lawyers qualify for the bar exam after an LL.M.

If You Can’t Work in Your Field Right Away

Re-licensing can take a year or more. Many professionals keep their careers moving with an adjacent role while they work through it — a foreign-trained doctor working as a clinical research coordinator, an engineer working as a technician or CAD designer, or an accountant working as a bookkeeper or staff accountant. Community colleges also offer short certificates that bridge the gap.

Upwardly Global is a nonprofit that helps immigrant professionals restart their careers in the US for free, with coaching, training and employer connections.

Put It on Your Resume

Once you have the evaluation, show it clearly: "Bachelor of Science in Computer Science (US equivalent, WES) — Technical University of Moldova, 2016." Recruiters stop wondering and start reading. For more, see How to Write a US-Style Resume.

Start the Paperwork Early

The slowest part of credential recognition is almost always getting documents from your home university. Find out exactly what your target employer, school or board needs, request official transcripts right away, and choose an evaluator they accept. A few weeks of paperwork can make your education count in the US for the rest of your career.
