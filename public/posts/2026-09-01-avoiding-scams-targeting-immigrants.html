How to Spot and Avoid Scams

Scammers deliberately target newcomers: people who are still learning how US agencies work, who may worry about their immigration status, and who are often sending money to family abroad. The good news is that almost every scam follows the same pattern. Once you learn the red flags, they become easy to spot.

The Red Flags Almost Every Scam Shares

  • Urgency or threats — "pay today or you will be arrested / deported / lose your account."
  • Unusual payment methods — gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, Zelle to a stranger, or cash handed to a courier. No real government agency asks for these.
  • Secrecy — "do not tell your bank," "do not hang up," "do not talk to your family."
  • Too good to be true — guaranteed investment returns, a job that pays well for almost no work, an apartment far below market price.
  • Unexpected contact — a call, text or message you did not ask for. Caller ID and email addresses are easy to fake.
Golden rule: if someone contacts you and asks for money or personal information, hang up and contact the organization yourself using a phone number or website you find independently — on your card, a bill, or the official .gov website.

Government Impersonation Scams

Fake IRS

The IRS normally contacts you first by mail. It never demands immediate payment by gift card, wire or crypto, and never threatens to have you arrested. See IRS scam alerts.

Fake Social Security

"Your Social Security Number has been suspended" is always a lie — SSNs are not suspended. The real SSA will never ask you to move money to "protect" it.

Fake USCIS

Calls claiming there is a problem with your visa or green card that can be fixed with a payment are scams. USCIS does not take payments by phone. Check your real case status in your USCIS online account.

"Notario" and Fake Immigration Help

In Moldova and much of Europe, a notary is a trained legal professional. In the US, a notary public can only witness signatures — they cannot give legal advice or file immigration cases. Unlicensed "consultants" have cost immigrants thousands of dollars and, worse, damaged their cases.

Only licensed attorneys and DOJ-accredited representatives at recognized nonprofit organizations may give immigration legal advice. Find accredited help through the Department of Justice’s legal representation resources, and confirm an Illinois lawyer’s license on the ARDC lawyer search. More warnings are on the USCIS scams page.

Job and Money Scams

  • Fake job offers that require you to pay for training, equipment, or a "background check" before you start.
  • Fake check scams — someone sends you a check, asks you to deposit it and send part of the money back. The check bounces days later, and you owe the bank the full amount.
  • Reshipping jobs — receiving packages at home and forwarding them abroad. The goods are usually bought with stolen cards.
  • Investment "mentors" who befriend you online or in a group chat, then guide you into a crypto or trading platform. The profits you see on screen are fake, and the money cannot be withdrawn.

Rental Scams

Watch for listings priced well below similar apartments, landlords who are "out of the country" and cannot show the unit, and requests to pay a deposit by Zelle, wire or gift card before you have seen the apartment or signed a lease. Never pay until you have toured the unit and verified who owns it — Cook County property records are public. More in Renting Your First Apartment.

Text, Email and Payment-App Scams

  • Fake text alerts about unpaid tolls, failed package deliveries, or a "locked" bank account — with a link to a fake payment page. Real companies rarely text you a payment link out of the blue.
  • Bank impersonation calls that ask you to read back a one-time code. Never share a verification code with anyone — that code is the key to your account.
  • Zelle, Venmo and Cash App payments work like cash. Once sent, they are very hard to reverse. Only send money to people you know and trust.

Scams Inside Our Own Community

Some of the most effective scams come from people who speak your language. Affinity fraud uses trust within a community — Romanian- or Russian-language groups on WhatsApp, Telegram or Facebook — to promote fake investment clubs, "guaranteed" visa or green card help, or money-exchange deals. Speaking Romanian does not make someone trustworthy. Apply the same red flags to everyone.

If You Think You’ve Been Scammed

Act quickly — the first hours matter most.

  • Stop all contact and payments with the scammer
  • Call your bank or card issuer immediately using the number on the back of your card
  • Change your passwords and turn on two-factor authentication
  • Freeze your credit for free at Equifax, Experian and TransUnion
  • Report it to the FTC at reportfraud.ftc.gov — and to the FBI at ic3.gov for online fraud
  • If your identity was stolen, make a recovery plan at identitytheft.gov
  • File a police report and keep the report number
  • Warn friends and family — scammers often target the same community again

Slow Down

Scams work by rushing you. Real agencies, banks and employers will give you time to verify. When anything feels urgent, secret, or too good to be true, stop, hang up, and check with someone you trust. There is no shame in being targeted — reporting it protects the next person.
