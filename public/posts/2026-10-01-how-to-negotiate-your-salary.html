How to Negotiate Your Salary

In many Eastern European workplaces, negotiating a job offer can feel rude or risky — you take what is offered and are grateful for it. In the US, it is the opposite: employers expect you to negotiate and usually leave room for it. A polite, well-prepared counteroffer is a normal part of the process and almost never costs you the job.

Why It Matters So Much

Your starting salary is the base for every future raise and often your next offer. Negotiating just $5,000 more at the start, with typical 3% annual raises, adds up to roughly $238,000 more over a 30-year career — before counting a higher 401(k) match or bonuses calculated as a percentage of salary.

Research Your Market Value

Go into every conversation knowing what the role pays in your city:

  • Levels.fyi — detailed salary, bonus and stock data, especially for tech
  • Glassdoor and LinkedIn Salary — ranges by company, title and location
  • The US Bureau of Labor Statistics — official wage data by occupation and metro area
  • Job postings — and in Illinois, this is now easy (see below)
  • People in your network — ask, "What range would you expect for this role?" rather than "What do you earn?"
Illinois law is on your side: since January 1, 2025, Illinois employers with 15 or more employees must include the pay scale and benefits in job postings. Illinois employers are also prohibited from asking about your salary history, so you never have to reveal what you earned before — including in Moldova.

Answering "What Are Your Salary Expectations?"

Recruiters often ask this in the first call. Early in the process, it is fine to stay flexible:

"I’d like to learn more about the role and the team first, but I’m confident we can find a number that works. Could you share the budgeted range for this position?"

If they insist, give a researched range where the bottom of your range is a number you would be happy with:

"Based on my research for similar roles in Chicago, I’m targeting $95,000 to $110,000 in base salary, depending on the full benefits package."

When the Offer Arrives

  1. Thank them and show enthusiasm — "I’m really excited about this opportunity."
  2. Ask for the offer in writing and for a little time — 24 to 72 hours is a normal request.
  3. Counter with a specific number and a reason — your research, your experience, a competing offer if you genuinely have one.
  4. Negotiate the whole package, not just base salary (see below).
  5. Get the final agreement in writing before you resign from your current job.

A simple counteroffer script:

"Thank you — I’m excited about the team and the work. Based on my research and my 8 years of experience in this area, I was expecting something closer to $105,000. Is there flexibility on the base salary?"

Then stop talking and let them respond. Silence is part of negotiating.

What Else You Can Negotiate

Sign-on Bonus

Easier for companies to approve than a higher salary. Useful if you are leaving a bonus or unvested stock behind.

Equity / RSUs

At tech and public companies, stock grants can be a large part of pay. Ask about the vesting schedule and refresh grants.

Time & Flexibility

Extra PTO days, remote or hybrid work, a later start date, or a flexible schedule.

Title & Level

A higher level often unlocks a higher pay band now and faster growth later.

Visa & Green Card

If you need sponsorship, ask the company to commit in writing to sponsoring your visa and to a timeline for starting the green card (PERM) process.

Learning & Relocation

A training budget, certification costs, or relocation support if you are moving for the job.

Common Mistakes

  • Accepting on the spot — even if the offer is great, take a day to review it.
  • Inventing a competing offer — recruiters talk, and it can backfire badly.
  • Negotiating against yourself — lowering your number before they have even responded.
  • Making it about personal needs — "my rent is high" is weaker than "the market rate for this role is…".
  • Apologizing or over-explaining — be confident, brief and friendly.

Asking for a Raise Later

Negotiation does not end when you accept. Keep a running list of your accomplishments with numbers, ask your manager what the criteria for the next level are, and raise compensation before the annual review cycle — budgets are usually set early. Read more about reviews and 1:1s in Understanding US Work Culture.

Ask — Politely and Prepared

The worst realistic outcome of a polite negotiation is hearing "this is our best offer." The best outcome is thousands of dollars a year for the rest of your career. Do your research, ask for a specific number, consider the whole package, and get it in writing.
